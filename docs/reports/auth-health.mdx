A remote scan runs for a long time against a target it authenticated with once,
at the start. Sessions expire. Cookies get rotated. Accounts get logged out. And
when that happens, the scan does not look broken.

It keeps sending requests. The target keeps answering `401`. The scan records
those as blocked probe rows, finishes on schedule, and produces a report that
looks exactly like a healthy one. Nothing on the scan row says the assessment
spent most of its budget talking to a login page.

Auth health tracking exists to make that failure loud.

## What counts as degradation

The tracker treats a route as newly failing when it answers `401` or `403` now
and either:

- answered `2xx` **earlier in this same scan**, or
- was successfully probed by an **earlier scan of the same project**

Either signal alone is enough, provided the route was recently working. The
second one matters because it catches the common case: a fresh scan inheriting
a cookie the previous scan already knew was stale.

It also refuses to speak up too early. A burst of at least five blocked probes
inside a five-minute window is required, and at least one of them must sit on a
route known to have worked earlier in the scan.

That last condition is not fussiness. Eight never-before-seen routes answering
`401` on a target turned out to be correct behaviour, and a target that
legitimately rejects a handful of mis-guessed URLs early on should not condemn
the whole scan. Older observations roll off the window for the same reason.

## What happens when it degrades

Three things, immediately:

1. A warning banner appears on the report.
2. The scan records a `degraded` state with the reason, so it survives the run
   and is visible on the scan afterwards.
3. The operator is notified that the scan needs a session.

Only `degraded` is persisted. A scan that never degraded does not need a row
saying it was fine.

## What happens next is your policy

| Policy | Behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Continue | The scan keeps going. You are told, and you decide. |
| Hold then wrap | The coordinator is steered off unauthenticated work, and if the session stays dead the scan wraps up early rather than spending the rest of its budget pointlessly |
| Wrap immediately | The scan stops as soon as degradation is confirmed |

Continue is the default, and it is a defensible default: a `401` on some routes
does not mean the whole target has stopped authenticating, and ending a scan
early loses real work.

## Refreshing the session mid-scan

You can update the session without restarting the scan. A verified re-login
clears the degraded verdict and the scan carries on from where it was.

This is usually the cheapest outcome. The work completed before the session
expired is not thrown away, and the report still covers the whole period rather
than only the part after you noticed.

The scan timeline keeps every transition — when it degraded, when someone
refreshed the session, when it wrapped — so the record reads in order rather than
as a single final verdict.

## Reading it on the report

When a scan degraded, the banner is the first thing to read before trusting a
clean result. A report with no findings from an authenticated scan that degraded
two minutes in is a report about a login page, not about the application.

For that reason the remote-scan review checklist includes whether the session
held. See [Remote target scans](/scans/remote-targets).

## How it stays safe

The tracker never raises. A detection failure must not break a probe, which is
the same rule the discovery auto-record follows — the health signal is important,
but it is not worth failing a scan over.
